Today covers the analytical and planning techniques: the ones that turn architecture descriptions into a realistic, prioritized plan. They are used mostly from Phase B onwards, and especially in Phases E and F.

1. Gap Analysis in More Depth

Day 5 introduced the gap analysis matrix: Baseline building blocks as rows, Target building blocks as columns, with a "New" row and an "Eliminated" column. When you look for gaps, check every domain:

DomainTypical gaps to look for
BusinessPeople (skills, staffing), process, tools, information, measurement, financial, facilities
DataData not current, not where it's needed, not available, not created, or not consumed; missing relationships
ApplicationApplications impacted, eliminated or newly created
TechnologyTechnology impacted, eliminated or newly created
Gap analysis is also a quality check: it reveals services or functions that were accidentally left out of the Target, and highlights opportunities for reuse.

2. Migration Planning Techniques

These techniques are used mainly in Phases E and F:

TechniquePurpose
Implementation Factor Assessment and Deduction matrixRecords the factors that affect implementation (risks, issues, assumptions, dependencies), in three columns: factor, description and deduction (what it means for the plan)
Consolidated Gaps, Solutions and Dependencies matrixLists every gap from Phases B–D with potential solutions and their dependencies, as the basis for work packages
Architecture Definition Increments tableShows which projects deliver which increments of the architecture across Transition Architectures
Transition Architecture State Evolution tableShows the state of each building block in each Transition Architecture
Business Value AssessmentPlots each work package on a value vs risk matrix to support prioritization

3. Interoperability Requirements

Interoperability is the ability to share information and services. TOGAF asks architects to define, as early as possible, the degree to which information and services must be shared.

Three categories

  • Operational or business interoperability: how business processes are shared
  • Information interoperability: how information is shared
  • Technical interoperability: how technical services are shared, or at least connect to one another

Four degrees of information interoperability

DegreeNameMeaning
1Unstructured data exchangeExchange of human-readable, unstructured data, such as free text
2Structured data exchangeExchange of structured data that still needs manual and/or automated handling
3Seamless sharing of dataAutomated sharing of data among systems based on a common exchange model
4Seamless sharing of informationUniversal interpretation of information, through cooperating applications

Interoperability is identified in Phase A, refined in Phases B to D, and consolidated and reconciled in Phase E.

4. Business Transformation Readiness Assessment

Used mainly in Phase A and revisited in Phases E and F, this technique evaluates how ready the organization is to change. TOGAF's readiness factors include:

VisionDesire, willingness and resolveNeed
Business caseFundingSponsorship and leadership
GovernanceAccountabilityWorkable approach and execution model
IT capacity to executeDepartmental capacity to executeAbility to implement and operate

The steps

  1. Determine the readiness factors
  2. Present them, for example using maturity models
  3. Assess each factor, rating its urgency, readiness status and degree of difficulty to fix
  4. Assess the risks for each factor and identify improvement actions
  5. Work those actions into the Implementation and Migration Plan (Phases E and F)

5. Risk Management

Risk is identified in Phase A and managed throughout the ADM. TOGAF's process:

  1. Risk classification
  2. Risk identification (the readiness factors are a good starting list)
  3. Initial risk assessment: classify each risk by effect and frequency
  4. Risk mitigation and residual risk assessment
  5. Risk monitoring, notably during implementation governance
ScaleValues
EffectCatastrophic · Critical · Marginal · Negligible
FrequencyFrequent · Likely · Occasional · Seldom · Unlikely
Resulting impactExtremely high · High · Moderate · Low
Initial risk is the level before mitigation; residual risk is what remains after mitigation. Exam questions often swap the two.

6. Capability-Based Planning

Capability-Based Planning is a business-planning technique that focuses on the planning, engineering and delivery of strategic business capabilities to the enterprise.
  • Capabilities are business-driven and often cut across organizational boundaries, so planning by capability helps avoid silos.
  • Capabilities are delivered in capability increments, each giving a visible, measurable business outcome.
  • Increments line up naturally with Transition Architectures: each transition delivers one or more increments.
  • In the 10th Edition this topic is covered in Series Guide material rather than the ADM Techniques document.

The takeaway

  • Gap analysis checks people, process, data, application and technology gaps, and doubles as a quality check.
  • Migration planning techniques include the Implementation Factor Assessment and Deduction matrix, the Consolidated Gaps, Solutions and Dependencies matrix and the Business Value Assessment.
  • Interoperability: business, information, technical categories and four degrees, consolidated in Phase E.
  • Readiness assessment rates factors by urgency, readiness status and difficulty, and feeds actions into the migration plan.
  • Risk: effect × frequency gives impact; know initial vs residual.
  • Capability-Based Planning delivers business capabilities in increments that align with Transition Architectures.
