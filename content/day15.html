2. Stakeholder Management

Stakeholder management is used mainly in the Preliminary Phase and Phase A, and is refined throughout the ADM. Its benefits include identifying the most powerful stakeholders early, gaining their support, anticipating reactions, and planning communication.

The four steps

  1. Identify stakeholders
  2. Classify stakeholder positions (their influence, interest and attitude)
  3. Determine the stakeholder management approach
  4. Tailor engagement deliverables: decide which views, artifacts and communications each stakeholder needs

Typical stakeholder groups include corporate functions (e.g. executives, finance, HR), the end-user organization, the project organization, system operations, and external parties such as suppliers and regulators.

The power/interest grid

Low interestHigh interest
High powerKeep SatisfiedKey Players: fully engage
Low powerMinimal Effort: monitorKeep Informed

The results are recorded in the Stakeholder Map (the Stakeholder Map Matrix artifact), which lists each stakeholder, their involvement, class, concerns, and the views and artifacts relevant to them.

3. Architecture Patterns

A pattern is an idea that has been useful in one practical context and will probably be useful in others. In architecture, a pattern describes a proven solution to a recurring problem, within a particular context.

A pattern is usually documented with elements such as:

ElementContent
NameA short, meaningful label
ProblemThe problem the pattern solves
ContextThe preconditions under which the problem and solution apply
ForcesThe constraints and trade-offs that must be balanced
SolutionHow to solve the problem
Resulting contextThe state of the system after the pattern is applied
Examples, rationale, related patterns, known usesEvidence and links that help others reuse the pattern
Patterns vs building blocks: building blocks are the what, the components you combine. Patterns explain how, when and why to combine them, and what trade-offs that involves.

4. Business Scenarios

A business scenario describes a business process, application or set of applications, the business and technology environment, the people and computing components (actors) involved, and the desired outcome of proper execution.

Business scenarios are used mainly in Phase A to discover and document the business requirements behind the Architecture Vision. They can be revisited in Phase B and in Requirements Management.

Developing a business scenario

  1. Identify, document and rank the problem driving the scenario
  2. Identify the business and technical environment and document it in scenario models
  3. Identify and document the desired objectives
  4. Identify the human actors and their place in the business model
  5. Identify the computer actors and their place in the technology model
  6. Document roles, responsibilities and measures of success for each actor
  7. Check fitness for purpose and refine only if necessary

Objectives should be SMART: Specific, Measurable, Actionable, Realistic and Time-bound.

5. Architecture Alternatives and Trade-Offs

There is rarely one obvious architecture. During Phases A to D, architects often develop several candidate architectures and help stakeholders choose between them:

  1. Identify alternatives that could meet the requirements
  2. Evaluate each against agreed criteria, such as cost, risk, time, fit with principles and stakeholder concerns
  3. Present the trade-offs openly to stakeholders
  4. Select an alternative with stakeholder agreement and record the rationale, for example in the Architecture Definition Document
The architect does not quietly pick the "best" option alone. Trade-offs are made visible and the choice is agreed with stakeholders.

The takeaway

  • Stakeholder management has four steps: identify, classify, determine approach, tailor deliverables.
  • Power/interest grid: Key Players, Keep Satisfied, Keep Informed, Minimal Effort.
  • A pattern is a proven solution to a recurring problem in a context; patterns say how to combine building blocks.
  • Business scenarios discover requirements, mainly in Phase A, with SMART objectives.
  • Alternatives and trade-offs are evaluated against criteria and chosen with stakeholders.
